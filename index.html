Every sector grew, but not at the same pace

Between 2006 and 2025, school enrolments rose by 23%, and every sector gained students. Government schools still added the most, about 343,000. But Independent schools grew four times as fast: up 62%, against 22% for Catholic schools and 15% for government schools.

The gap has widened since 2020. Government enrolments have levelled off, while Independent schools added another 117,000 students in five years. Yet government schools still teach more than three times as many students as either other sector.

Faster growth has shifted the mix. Of every 100 school students in 2006, 13 were at an Independent school. By 2025 it was 17.

The government share fell from 67 to 63 in every 100, while Catholic schools held steady at 20.

Independent schools are gaining ground everywhere

The shift towards Independent schools is not confined to one state. Since 2006 their share has grown in every state and territory, and nationally the shift has sped up. It rose from 14.4% to 15.0% between 2015 and 2020, then to 17.2% by 2025.

In 2006, only South Australia and Western Australia had more than 14% of students at Independent schools. By 2025 every state was above that mark, and New South Wales had climbed from 11.7% to 16.6%.

Share alone hides scale. South Australia has the highest Independent share, at 20%. But New South Wales teaches 30% of the nation's students, so its 17% share means about 209,000 Independent students, against 56,000 in South Australia.

209,000Independent students in New South Wales, nearly four times South Australia's 56,000

Over the last decade the biggest rise came in New South Wales, where the Independent share went from 13.0% to 16.6% of students. Queensland and Tasmania were close behind.

The Northern Territory is the only place where the Independent share fell, from 16.1% to 14.8%.

13% to 17%of New South Wales students at Independent schools, 2015 to 2025: the largest rise of any state

Year 7 is when families switch sectors

Much of that shift happens at one moment: the move to high school. In 2025, 67% of Year 6 students were at government schools, but only 56% of Year 7 students.

Following the same students from Year 6 in 2024 to Year 7 in 2025, government schools had about 37,500 fewer of them. Catholic schools gained about 17,000 and Independent schools about 23,000.

This is not new. In every year since 2006, the government share has dropped at Year 7, and the drop has deepened: the government share of Year 7 fell from 65% in 2006 to 56% in 2025. In Queensland and Western Australia, where the data counts Year 7 as primary school until 2014, there was almost no Year 7 drop in 2006.

Victoria, the ACT and the West grew fastest

Where those students go to school has changed as much as which schools they choose. Between 2015 and 2025 the ACT grew fastest (18%), then Victoria (16%) and Western Australia (15%). New South Wales grew only 6%, while Tasmania and the Northern Territory shrank slightly.

Fast growth in a small state adds few students. Victoria alone added 148,000 students, more than a third of the national increase of 410,000.

New South Wales, the biggest state, added half as many. Tasmania and the Northern Territory ended the decade with slightly fewer students.

Growth lives on the edge of our cities

Within each state, growth is more uneven still. Of the 9,156 schools open in both 2015 and 2025, exactly half now have fewer students than a decade ago. Together, those in major cities added about 225,000 students.

By contrast, schools in outer regional, remote and very remote areas lost about 12,900 students. Within the cities, the growth sits on the outskirts: in Melbourne, the fastest-growing schools cluster in the outer west and south-east.

Since 2015, 538 new schools have opened, and almost three in four are in a major city. Many new Independent schools are small: the typical one has 75 students, so together they hold only 36,500 of the 238,000 students at new schools.

538new schools since 2015: 264 government, 212 Independent and 62 Catholic

New schools are not all alike. A typical new primary school already has 425 students, well above the 252 at a typical older primary. New secondary and combined schools are still small.

425 vs 252students at a typical new primary school, against a typical primary already open in 2015

Wyndham, in Melbourne's outer west, added 29,300 students. More than half of them, 17,200, are at schools that did not exist in 2015.

Only the City of Brisbane added more. Nine of these 12 council areas are on the outer edges of Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane.

Beyond the cities, government schools carry the load

Away from the cities, the sector story reverses. The further from a major city, the more students rely on government schools.

They teach 62% of students in major cities and inner regional areas, 69% in outer regional areas, 76% in remote areas and 88% in very remote areas.
