How Australia's School Enrolments Changed, 2006–2025

Australia has 767,000 more school students than 20 years ago.

This page asks two questions about them. First, which schools are teaching them: which sectors grew, and when families move between them. Second, where they are: which states, suburbs and regions took on the growth. It draws on ABS enrolment counts for every year from 2006 to 2025, and on ACARA figures for more than 9,600 individual schools, which compare each school in 2015 with 2025.

Part 1 · Which schools

Every sector grew, but not at the same pace

Between 2006 and 2025, school enrolments rose by 23%, and every sector gained students. Government schools still added the most, about 343,000, but Independent schools grew four times as fast: up 62%, against 22% for Catholic schools and 15% for Government schools.

The gap has widened since 2020. Government enrolments have levelled off, while Independent schools added another 117,000 students in just five years. The two charts show the same students two ways: Independent schools lead on growth, but Government schools still teach more than three times as many students as either other sector.

Faster growth has shifted the mix. Of every 100 school students in 2006, 13 were enrolled at an Independent school; by 2025 it was 17. Government schools' share fell from 67 to 63, while Catholic schools held steady at 20.

Independent schools are gaining ground everywhere

The shift towards Independent schools is not confined to one state. Since 2006, Independent schools have taken a bigger share of students in every state and territory, and nationally the shift has sped up: the Independent share rose from 14.4% to 15.0% between 2015 and 2020, then to 17.2% by 2025.

In 2006, Independent schools taught more than 14% of students in only South Australia and Western Australia. By 2025 every state was above that mark, and New South Wales, once second-lowest at 11.7%, had reached 16.6%.

Share alone hides scale. South Australia has the highest Independent share, at 20%, but New South Wales teaches 30% of the nation's students, so its 17% share adds up to about 209,000 Independent students, against 56,000 in South Australia.

Over the last decade the biggest gains came in New South Wales (up 3.6 percentage points), Queensland (3.3) and Tasmania (3.1). The Northern Territory is the only place where the Independent share fell, from 16.1% to 14.8%.

Year 7 is when families switch sectors

Much of that shift happens at one moment: the move to high school. In 2025, 67% of Year 6 students were at government schools, but only 56% of Year 7 students. Following the same students from Year 6 in 2024 to Year 7 in 2025, government schools had about 37,500 fewer of them, while Catholic schools gained about 17,000 and Independent schools about 23,000.

Each square shows the share of students at government schools for one year level in one year; paler squares mean more students at Catholic and Independent schools. The pale band starts at Year 7 in every year since 2006, and it has deepened: the government share of Year 7 students fell from 65% in 2006 to 56% in 2025. In Queensland and Western Australia, where the data counts Year 7 as primary school until 2014, there was almost no Year 7 drop in 2006. Choose a state to compare.

Part 2 · Where

Victoria, the ACT and the West grew fastest

Growth was just as uneven across the map. Between 2015 and 2025 the ACT grew fastest (18%), followed by Victoria (16%) and Western Australia (15%), while New South Wales grew only 6% and Tasmania and the Northern Territory shrank slightly. Move the slider to compare other years, or click a state to see its sectors.

Fast growth in a small state adds few students. Victoria alone added 148,000 students between 2015 and 2025, more than a third of the national increase of 410,000. New South Wales, the biggest state, added half as many, while Tasmania and the Northern Territory ended the decade with slightly fewer students than they started with.

Growth lives on the edge of our cities

Within each state, growth is more uneven still. Of the 9,156 schools open in both 2015 and 2025, exactly half now have fewer students than they did a decade ago. The growth is concentrated on the outskirts of the capital cities.

Each circle is one school, sized by its students and coloured by how much it has grown. Zoom to Melbourne and the purple clusters sit in the outer west and south-east: in Tarneit, Good News Lutheran College has grown from 676 to 1,872 students since 2015. Search for any school or suburb to find it on the map.

Since 2015, 538 new schools have opened: 264 government, 212 Independent and 62 Catholic, and almost three in four of them are in a major city. Click Independent in the legend: many new Independent schools are small, with a typical enrolment of 75 students, so they hold only 36,500 of the 238,000 students now at new schools.

New schools are not all alike. A typical new primary school already has 425 students, well above the 252 at a typical older primary. New secondary and combined schools are still small.

Wyndham, in Melbourne's outer west, added 29,300 students, and more than half of them (17,200) are at schools that did not exist in 2015. Only the City of Brisbane added more. Nine of these 12 council areas are on the outer edges of Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane.

Beyond the cities, government schools carry the load

Away from the cities, the sector story reverses.

The further from a major city, the more students rely on government schools. They teach 62% of students in major cities and inner regional areas, 69% in outer regional areas, 76% in remote areas and 88% in very remote areas.

What it adds up to

The national total of 767,000 extra students hides two shifts: towards Independent schools, and towards the edges of the biggest cities.

  • Independent schools grew fastest. Up 62% since 2006, they now teach 17 in every 100 students, up from 13.
  • Year 7 is the turning point. The government share falls from 67% in Year 6 to 56% in Year 7.
  • Victoria took the most growth. It added 148,000 students between 2015 and 2025, more than a third of the national increase.
  • The suburbs are where it lands. Half of all schools lost students, while council areas like Wyndham added nearly 30,000.
  • Remote Australia depends on government schools. They teach 88% of students in very remote areas.

Sources: ABS Schools 2025; ACARA Data Access Program.
